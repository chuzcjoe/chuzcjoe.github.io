# 1. What is Roth?

A Roth account is a type of retirement saving plan where contributions are made from after-tax dollars. Unlike traditional retirement accounts, Roth accounts allow for tax-free growth and withdrawals, provided certain conditions are met, making them an excellent choice for long-term savings. The most common types of Roth accounts are the **Roth IRA** (Individual Retirement Account) and the **Roth 401(k)**.

Key Features:
- **Tax-free growth:** Once your money is in a Roth account, it grows tax-free.
- **Tax-free withdrawals:** Qualified withdrawals are completely tax-free.

# 2. Roth Types

## 2.1 Roth IRA

A **Roth IRA** (Individual Retirement Account) is a type of retirement savings account to which you contribute after-tax dollars. It offers tax-free growth and tax-free withdrawals in retirement.

**Eligibility**
- Who can open a Roth IRA? 

Anyone can open a Roth IRA. There is no age limit to open a Roth IRA. You must have earned income in the year to start contribution, which means even people under 18 who earn money from a part-time job can satrt saving for retirement. Most of the major brokerage firms offer choices for opening a Roth IRA and a wide range of investment options such as Fidelity, Vanguard, E*TRADE, etc.

- Who can contribute to Roth IRA?

Those with earned income within the IRS's annual limits are eligible to contribute. Your ability to contribute to a Roth IRA depends on your **modified adjusted gross income(MAGI)**. Figure 1 belows shows the contribution limit for 2024.

For those who are eligible to contribute to Roth IRA, there is also age difference. For example, in 2024, the Roth IRA contribution limit is `$7000` for those under 50, and an additional `$1000` catch-up contribution for those 50 and older, in total, it would be `$8000`.

**Tax Advantages and Withdrawal Rules**
- Tax-free growth and withdrawals.
- Contributions can be withdrawn at any time without penalties. Ensure the amount is within your total contributions. For example, Fidelity keeps track of your contributions and earnings. You can find this information in your annual tax forms.
- Earnings are tax-free if withdrawn after age 59½ and the account has been open for 5+ years. A 10% early withdrawal penalty will be added if you’re under 59½ and don’t qualify for an exception.

## 2.2 Roth 401k

A Roth 401(k) is a type of `employer-sponsored` retirement savings account. It allows you to make contributions with after-tax dollars, and your investments grow tax-free. When you withdraw the money in retirement, both the contributions and earnings are tax-free, provided you meet certain conditions.

- Contributions are made with after-tax dollars.
- Qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free, including both contributions and earnings.

**Eligibility**

Any employee whose employer offers a Roth 401(k) option as part of its retirement plan. Unlike a Roth IRA, there is no income limit for the contribution.

**Contribution limits**

Compared to Roth IRA contribution, Roth 401k has higher contribution limit.

**Employer Matching**

Most employers offer both Traditional 401K and Roth 401k retirement plans. Some employers can match contributions, but the match `goes into a traditional 401(k) (pre-tax) account`, regardless of whether your contributions are pre-tax or Roth, which will be taxed upon withdrawal. The range of 401(k) matching percentages varies widely depending on the employer, typical match is around `3% to 6%`.

For example, in 2024, the Roth 401(k) contribution limit is `$23,000` for employee contributions and `$69,000` for employee and employer contributions combined. There's also a `$7,500` catch-up contribution for those age 50 and older, which raises the employee limit to `$30,500` for those eligible.

**RMDs**

RMD refers to `Required Minimum Dsitributions`, which is the minimum amount of money that individuals must withdraw from their retirement accounts, such as traditional IRAs or 401(k) plans, once they reach a certain age. The IRS mandates these withdrawals to ensure that tax-deferred retirement savings are eventually taxed.
